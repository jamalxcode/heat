About these yield curves

heat.sala.company/rates shows government bond yields for the United States, the euro area, the United Kingdom, Japan and Canada: each market's 10-year yield, how it moved, and its whole yield curve, with a simple reading of whether the curve looks healthy.

What is a yield curve?

The interest rate a government pays to borrow for different lengths of time, from a few months to 30 years, drawn as one line. Normally it slopes up: lenders want more to lock their money away for longer.

How do I know if a yield curve is healthy?

Look at the gap between the 10-year and the 2-year yield (for the UK, the Bank of England's Bank Rate). Half a percentage point or more is normal; between zero and half a point is flat; below zero, with short-term rates above long-term ones, the curve is inverted. An inverted US curve has come before most US recessions, though the timing varies and it is not a guarantee.

Where does the data come from, and how fresh is it?

Only from official sources: the US Treasury, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, Japan's Ministry of Finance and the Bank of Canada. They publish closing yields once a business day, some a few days late, so each market shows its own date. Nothing here is real-time.

Why aren't Saudi Arabia, the UAE or other Gulf markets shown?

Their central banks publish auction results, not a daily yield curve, and no free official daily source exists yet. China and India are missing for the same reason. Morocco's central bank does publish a daily curve, but its website blocks the automated servers this site runs on.

Is this financial advice?

No. It is an experimental, work-in-progress tool. Data can be late, wrong or missing.
