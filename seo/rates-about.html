About these yield curves

heat.sala.company/rates shows government bond yields for the United States, the euro area, Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada, Australia, Switzerland, Sweden, Norway, Brazil, China, India and South Africa, plus monthly 10-year yields for 15 more EU countries (France, Italy, Spain and others): each market's 10-year yield, how it moved, and its whole yield curve, with a simple reading of whether the curve looks healthy.

What is a yield curve?

The interest rate a government pays to borrow for different lengths of time, from a few months to 30 years, drawn as one line. Normally it slopes up: lenders want more to lock their money away for longer.

How do I know if a yield curve is healthy?

Look at the gap between the 10-year and the 2-year yield (for the UK, the Bank of England's Bank Rate; for South Africa, the 3-month bill). Half a percentage point or more is normal; between zero and half a point is flat; below zero, with short-term rates above long-term ones, the curve is inverted. Each country's gap is also ranked against its own last ten years, since some curves are usually steep and others usually flat. In the US an inverted curve has come before most recessions since the 1960s, months to two years ahead, though not every time; that record is American, so elsewhere read an inversion as markets expecting rates to fall.

What is a real yield?

The 10-year yield minus the latest yearly inflation: roughly what a lender earns after rising prices. It makes very different markets comparable: Brazil's yields are high partly because its inflation is, and Switzerland's are low partly because its inflation is low. The inflation figures come from Eurostat, the OECD, the South African Reserve Bank and Japan's Statistics Bureau.

Where does the data come from, and how fresh is it?

Only from official sources: the US Treasury, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, Japan's Ministry of Finance, the Bank of Canada, the Reserve Bank of Australia, the Bundesbank, the Swiss National Bank, the Riksbank, Norges Bank, Brazil's National Treasury, ChinaBond, Financial Benchmarks India and the South African Reserve Bank, plus the European Central Bank's monthly figures for the other EU countries. They publish closing yields once a business day, some a few days late, so each market shows its own date. Nothing here is real-time.

Why aren't Saudi Arabia, the UAE or other Gulf markets shown?

Their central banks publish auction results, not a daily yield curve, and no free official daily source exists yet. France, Italy, Spain and the other EU countries have no free daily source either, so they appear in a separate row with the European Central Bank's monthly 10-year yields, clearly marked monthly. Morocco's central bank does publish a daily curve, but its website blocks the automated servers this site runs on.

Is this financial advice?

No. It is an experimental, work-in-progress tool. Data can be late, wrong or missing.
